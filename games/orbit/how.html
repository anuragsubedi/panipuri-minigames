How Orbit works

Orbit is a small gravity simulator. Every body pulls on every other body, time moves forward in tiny fixed steps, and the numbers on screen are real units.

Open Orbit

Real units, in disguise

Inside, the simulation uses plain numbers. They are chosen so they line up with reality:

  • Distance: 1 unit is 0.01 AU (an AU is the Earth–Sun distance, about 150 million km). Earth sits 100 units from the Sun.
  • Mass: 1 unit is one-thousandth of the Sun. The Sun is 1000.
  • Time: with gravity's constant set to 1, those two choices fix the clock: 1 unit of time is 1.84 days. A body 100 units out takes exactly one year to go around.

That's why the clock, speeds (km/s), distances (AU) and masses (in Earths, Jupiters or Suns) mean what they say. At 1× speed, one real second is about 5 weeks; at 64× it's about 6 years.

Gravity

Each body is pulled toward every other body by Newton's law: the pull grows with mass and fades with the square of distance.

a = G · m / (d² + ε²)3/2 · (direction × d)

With up to 50 bodies that's 1225 pairs, worked out fresh every step. The small ε (softening, 0.015 AU) stops the pull from becoming infinite when two bodies pass very close, which would fling them off at absurd speeds.

Moving time forward

Time advances in fixed steps of 2.2 hours of sim time, using a method called leapfrog (kick, drift, kick):

  1. Nudge each velocity by half a step of gravity.
  2. Move each body along its velocity for a full step.
  3. Recompute gravity at the new positions and nudge velocities by the other half.

Leapfrog keeps an orbit's energy steady over thousands of laps instead of slowly spiralling in or out. The test suite checks that a circular orbit loses less than 1% of its energy over 20,000 steps.

Collisions

Two bodies touch when the distance between their centers is less than their radii added together. What happens next is your choice:

ModeWhat happensWhat's kept
MergeThey become one body at their shared center of mass. The heavier one keeps its name and colour.Total mass and momentum
BounceThey push apart along the line between them, losing a little speed (80% of the head-on speed survives).Total momentum

Bodies that drift more than 80 AU from the system's center are dropped.

Launching and the predicted path

When you drag, the arrow sets the new body's direction and speed. The dotted line is a forecast: the sim runs 300 quick steps ahead for the new body, treating everything else as frozen. The dots are evenly spaced in time, so where they bunch up the body is slow, and where they spread out it's fast. An ✕ means it's heading into something.

The real Solar system

The Solar system preset starts on 1 January 2000, using published orbital elements for each planet: how big its orbit is, how stretched it is (eccentricity), and where it was on that date. Orbit flattens everything to 2D and leaves out moons; our Moon sits closer to Earth than the softening distance.

The rest

  • Gravity and Sun mass multiply the real values (¼× to 4×) without touching velocities, so balanced orbits stop being balanced.
  • Unnamed bodies are labelled by real thresholds: rocky planet under 10 Earths, gas giant under 13 Jupiters, brown dwarf under 80 Jupiters, then star.
  • Saved systems stay in this browser. It's 2D, with no relativity, tides or moons, and bodies are drawn bigger than life.
