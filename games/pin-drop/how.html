panipuri / Pin Drop How it works

How Pin Drop works

You get a place name and drop a pin on a blank world map. The game measures how far your pin is from the real spot, along the curve of the Earth, and turns that into points.

Play Pin Drop

A round

  1. A clue appears: a place name, plus its country (or state, on a country map) unless you're on Hard. Landmarks have an i button that says what the place is, without saying where.
  2. Tap to drop your pin, drag it to adjust, and pinch or scroll to zoom in. Then lock it in.
  3. The map shows your pin, the real spot and a line between them, with the distance and your points.

A game is 5 or 10 rounds. The summary puts every round on one map.

Maps

Pick the World or a country map (so far India and Nepal). A country map shows its state or province borders and has its own topics.

Some borders are disputed. A country map draws them the way that country's official map does, and says so in the corner of the map. India's map uses Natural Earth's "point of view" data. The world map uses Natural Earth's default lines.

Nepal is the most detailed map: the Survey Department of Nepal's own boundaries (2024, via OCHA HDX, CC BY 3.0 IGO), so it includes Kalapani, Lipulekh and Limpiyadhura as Nepal's official map does. It shows the 7 provinces, all 77 districts and, as you zoom in, all 753 local levels (palikas), plus the big rivers. Its topics are Cities (every palika; rural ones only on Hard), Landmarks and Nature (parks, lakes, rivers, peaks).

A river, park or lake counts as its whole shape: drop the pin anywhere on the river's course in Nepal, or inside the park, and you're right on it. Otherwise the distance is to the nearest point of the shape, and the reveal highlights it.

Topics and difficulty

Topic and difficulty are separate. Every place has a fame tier: 1 for places most people could find, 3 for ones only geography fans know. A world capital can be tier 3 (Palikir) and a landmark can be tier 1 (the Eiffel Tower).

Capitals and cities come from Natural Earth (public domain); Nepal's palikas come from the Survey Department data above, placed at their town centre from OpenStreetMap. Landmarks are hand-picked, with coordinates (and river and park shapes) from OpenStreetMap (© OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL). On the world map, places whose capital or statehood is disputed are left out.

Tiers on country maps come from Wikidata: how many Wikipedia languages have an article about the place. That's a global signal, so it can only promote a landmark, never push a place famous at home down a tier.

Picking the places

The game shuffles every place allowed by your topic and difficulty, then works down the list. It skips any place in a region (Africa, Asia, Europe, North America, South America, Oceania) already used (on a country map, a zone of states or a province), until every region has had a turn. It does the same across topics in Mix. That way you don't get three European cities in a row, and Mix isn't mostly cities.

The map

The map is an equirectangular projection: longitude goes straight across, latitude straight up. A tap becomes a latitude and longitude with simple arithmetic, so no mapping library is needed. Country shapes are bundled with the game, so nothing loads over the network while you play.

The flat map stretches the far north and south. The reveal line is drawn straight on the flat map, not as the real curved shortest path, so a line across the Pacific can look odd. The distance itself is always the true one.

Distance

The haversine formula gives the great-circle distance between two points on a sphere of radius 6,371 km:

d = 2R · asin √( sin²(Δφ/2) + cos φ₁ · cos φ₂ · sin²(Δλ/2) )

φ is latitude and λ is longitude. London to Paris comes out at about 344 km.

Scoring

points = 10 × e−(km / 1500)

The scale belongs to each pack: 1,500 km on the world map, 250 km on India. 500 km off is great for the world and poor inside one country.
